## Post 1 — the empty elevator (Wed Sep 3)

Elevators used to have drivers.

For half a century, nobody would ride without one. Push-button elevators
existed by 1900. People stepped in, saw no operator, and stepped right
back out.

Then in 1945, New York's elevator operators went on strike, the city
bled millions a day, and buildings installed the buttons. Riders
grumbled, then adjusted, then forgot there had ever been a stool in the
corner.

Nobody went on strike against practice marketing. But I remember the
moment I realized the operator's seat was emptying. [Your real moment:

## Post 2 — the photographer question (Sat Sep 6)

When cameras went digital, photographers were supposed to disappear.

Film cost money. Every click was a decision. Then photos became free,
infinite, disposable. Everyone owned a camera. Everyone was a
photographer.

And yet: weddings still hire one.

Because the scarce thing was never the shutter. It was knowing where to
stand. Seeing the moment forming two seconds before it arrives. Being
trusted in the room on the biggest day of someone's life.

Software is having its digital-camera moment. [A real beat: something
that once took you months or serious money that now takes an afternoon.]
Working software has become almost free.

So I started asking the photographer question about local practices:
when production is free, what still costs something?

Three answers kept coming back.

Your voice. Patients spot generic content the way you spot a stock
photo.
Your evenings. A tool that adds review work steals the very thing it
promised to give back.
Your license. In healthcare, one sloppy claim outweighs a thousand good
posts.

None of those are production problems. They are trust problems. And
trust doesn't scale by shutter clicks.

The system I built starts from that premise. On Tuesday, I'll show you
its portfolio.

## Post 4 — the captain's signature (Thu Sep 11)

Autopilot has flown most of every flight you've ever taken.

It holds altitude better than any human. It doesn't get tired over the
Atlantic. And at the front of the plane there is still, always, a
captain.

Not because the technology needs babysitting. Because the flight needs
someone accountable. A signature. A person whose judgment, and whose
license, stand behind the machine's precision.

That's the design I stole for practice marketing.

My engine researches, writes, designs, schedules, distributes. It could
publish on its own. I built it so it can't. Nothing reaches the public
until the owner looks at it and presses approve. Five minutes, coffee in
hand. The machine does the eighty percent that eats your evenings. You
do the twenty percent only a licensed professional can: judgment.

In healthcare this isn't philosophy, it's survival. No outcome promises.
No invented patient stories. No claims your license can't carry. An AI
publishing unsupervised in this space isn't a productivity win. It's a
liability with a monthly fee.

Precision from the machine. Accountability from the captain.

Tuesday morning, the doors open.
